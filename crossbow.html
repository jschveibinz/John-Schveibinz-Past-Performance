Crossbow

Multi-Dimensional Signal Processing and Synthesis Fortran Software Suite

OrganizationSystems Engineering and Development Corporation (SEDC)
Work period1985–1990
ContributorJohn Schveibinz

Product overview

A Fortran software suite for synthesizing complex modulated waveforms and analyzing them through multidimensional, quadratic signal processing. Crossbow ran on VAX/VMS computers with VT220 terminals.

Work performed

Contributed to waveform synthesis; Wigner, cyclostationary, product-plane, cross-ambiguity-function, and cepstrum analyses; and the storage, transformations, extraction, printing, and export functions needed to produce analytical results. Developed algorithms using Crossbow and presented results to customers.

Software development; algorithm development; customer presentations

Engineering significance

Combined signal synthesis, complementary analysis representations, and data handling in a common engineering environment for investigating signal characteristics.

How 2D acousto-optic processing works

Acousto-optic processors use sound waves to encode electrical signals onto light. An electrical input drives a transducer on a Bragg cell, producing a traveling acoustic wave that changes the material’s refractive index. A laser beam passing through the cell is diffracted by this moving optical grating, transferring signal information into the optical field.

Arrangements of Bragg cells, lenses, and reference beams can perform signal multiplication, Fourier transformation, and correlation. A two-dimensional processor produces an analysis plane with two independent coordinates: for example, a cross-ambiguity plane can represent relative time delay on one axis and frequency offset on the other. A detector array records the optical output, with time-integrating architectures accumulating light at each detector position. Digitization makes the resulting plane available for storage and further analysis. The coordinates and processing operation depend on the optical architecture; a 2D camera alone does not make a processor mathematically two-dimensional. See the time-integrating processor patent and research on acousto-optic time-frequency representations for examples.

How Crossbow supported system development

At SEDC, Crossbow provided the software simulation and analysis side of work on acousto-optic processing systems. Its waveform synthesis and numerical analysis planes offered a way to calculate expected signal-processing results before comparing them with optical hardware outputs.

  • Design: Synthetic waveforms and complementary representations could help engineers explore which processing approach exposed the signal characteristics of interest and define expected analysis-plane features.
  • Development: Repeatable numerical reference results could help separate algorithm behavior from implementation effects when interpreting a prototype’s output.
  • Testing: Applying corresponding test signals to simulation and hardware would allow comparison of feature locations, shapes, and relative responses. Differences could guide investigation of calibration, optical alignment, finite bandwidth, detector integration, or digitization.

These examples explain the engineering value of Crossbow’s documented synthesis and analysis capabilities. They are an interpretation of how such simulation would support development, rather than a claim that Crossbow included a detailed model of every optical component or that every comparison procedure listed here was performed.
